
\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:intro}

Machine-learning (ML) models have reached state-of-the-art skill in weather
forecasting, rivaling traditional physics-based numerical models at a fraction
of their computational cost
\parencite{lam2023graphcast,bi2023pangu}.

An increasingly important class of these models is formed by generative
probabilistic weather models
\parencite{price2023gencast,couairon2024archesweather}.
Rather than producing a single deterministic forecast, these models are trained
to approximate the conditional distribution of future atmospheric states.
Sampling from the learned distribution enables the direct generation of
forecast ensembles and thereby an explicit representation of forecast
uncertainty.
This probabilistic formulation also mitigates the smoothing effects often
associated with deterministic models and improves probabilistic forecast skill,
measured for instance by the Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS)
\parencite{hersbach2000crps,price2023gencast}.

Beyond their use as probabilistic samplers, generative models can also be
actively controlled during generation. This is standard in image generation,
where applications such as text-to-image synthesis, editing, and inpainting
rely on \emph{guidance} to steer a pretrained generative model toward a desired
outcome
\parencite{dhariwal2021diffusion,ho2022classifierfree,nichol2021glide}.
Generative weather models, by contrast, are still predominantly used in their
unguided form, and their potential for controlled generation remains
comparatively unexplored.

A small number of recent studies have begun to investigate different forms of
weather control for the generation of extreme-event scenarios, including
tropical cyclones \parencite{brenowitz2025climate}, droughts
\parencite{ueyama2026guided}, and heatwaves
\parencite{whittaker2025heatwaves}. 
These approaches shift the focus from forecasting a distribution of likely
weather trajectories to generating plausible low-probability alternatives
under prescribed conditions of interest. We refer to this complementary task
as \emph{scenario generation}.

This distinction is particularly relevant for extreme-weather storylines.
Extreme events account for a disproportionate share of weather-related impacts
\parencite{wmo2023atlas}, yet their rarity means that both historical
observations and finite forecast ensembles provide only a limited sample of
possible realizations. Considering plausible low-probability alternatives
beyond those represented in historical observations and finite forecast
ensembles is therefore essential for assessing extreme-weather risk
\parencite{thompson2017unseen,shepherd2018storylines}.

We are therefore interested in exploiting the ability of generative weather models to produce coherent weather trajectories while deliberately steering them toward prescribed extreme events.
Transferring guidance methods from image generation to this setting is nonetheless not straightforward. 
First, while image-generation targets can often be expressed directly through a text prompt, class label, or reference image, weather scenarios require the conditioning objective to be explicitly constructed. Second, while image plausibility can often be assessed from visual coherence, weather plausibility is inherently multivariate and spatiotemporal: guided trajectories must remain coherent across space and pressure levels, preserve meaningful relationships between meteorological variables, and evolve realistically over time.

These considerations lead to the central research question of this thesis:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Can we guide generative probabilistic weather models to produce extreme
weather trajectories that satisfy a prescribed target while remaining
meteorologically plausible?}
\end{quote}

We address this question by separating two problems that are often intertwined
in guided generation: \emph{what} scenario should be generated, and \emph{how}
the generative model should be steered to produce it. We refer to these two
stages as \emph{Specify} and \emph{Guide}.

The first stage defines the desired scenario independently of the guidance
mechanism. In our setting, this amounts to specifying a target variable, a
spatial region, and a target trajectory describing how the corresponding
regional quantity should evolve over forecast time. The second stage then
determines how the generative dynamics must be modified to realize the
prescribed target.

This separation leads to a central distinction from the approaches most closely
related to ours. Existing guidance methods typically prescribe the strength of
the intervention and observe the resulting change in the generated scenario.
We instead specify the desired outcome first and adapt the guidance strength to
reach it. To do so, we parameterize guidance through a prescribed profile over
the flow and determine its overall magnitude separately for each
forecast step.

We investigate this approach using ArchesWeather
\parencite{couairon2024archesweather}, a medium-range probabilistic weather
model based on flow matching. As an experimental case study, we focus on
regional surface-temperature heatwaves and specify the target through a
prescribed temperature trajectory over a selected spatial region. The
underlying formulation is nevertheless not restricted to temperature: the same
approach can in principle be applied to other differentiable functionals of the
forecast state. More generally, although extreme weather provides the
application considered in this thesis, the proposed formulation can be used to
generate alternative weather trajectories toward any suitably specified
target.

The main contributions of this thesis are the following.

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item We provide a common formulation of guidance-strength schedules that
    separates the overall guidance magnitude from its distribution over
    generative flow time.

    \item We formulate the specification of a target weather scenario through a
    spatial mask and a scalar target trajectory, separating the desired outcome
    from the guidance mechanism used to realize it.

    \item We develop a target-driven gradient-based guidance method that
    prescribes the relative distribution of guidance over flow time and adapts
    its overall magnitude to the specified target.

    \item We systematically investigate how the placement of guidance over flow
    time affects the resulting scenarios and use the selected configuration to
    generate and evaluate extreme-weather storylines.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of the thesis is organized as follows.
\Cref{ch:background} introduces the technical background on flow matching and
guidance, reviews existing approaches to controlled weather generation, and
describes ArchesWeather. \Cref{ch:specify} develops the specification of target
weather scenarios, while \Cref{ch:guide} presents the guidance method used to
realize them. \Cref{ch:evaluation} introduces the criteria used to assess the
resulting trajectories, and \Cref{ch:experiments} presents the experimental
results, which are discussed in \Cref{ch:discussion}. Finally,
\Cref{ch:conclusion} summarizes the main findings and outlines directions for
future work.
