Generative modeling frames the task of generating objects $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$, for instance images or text, as sampling from a data distribution $p^1$.
In particular, flow matching \parencite{lipman2023flow} models the data distribution $p_1$ as the endpoint of a
continuous transport of a tractable prior distribution $p_0$, via the probability path $(p_t)_{t\in[0,1]}$.
This path is generated by a time-dependent velocity field $u_t$, defining the ordinary differential equation (ODE)
$$
    \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\,\phi_t(x) = u_t\big(\phi_t(x)\big), \qquad \phi_0(x) = x.
$$
where $\phi_t$ denotes the flow (trajectory) of $x$, whose push-forward carries $p_0$ to $p_t$.
% ; equivalently, $u_t$ and $p_t$ obey the continuity equation $\partial_t p_t + \nabla\!\cdot(p_t\,u_t) = 0$.
% Sampling then reduces to drawing $x_0 \sim p_0$ and integrating the flow to $t=1$. Generally the ODE has no closed form solution and is thus simulated numerically, for example via Euler's method.

A common choice for $(p_t)_{t\in[0,1]}$ is the Gaussian conditional probability path
$$
    p_t(\cdot \mid x_1) = \mathcal{N}\!\big(\alpha_t\, x_1,\ \beta_t^{2}\, I\big) \qquad \alpha_t: 0 \rightarrow 1, \quad \beta_t: 1 \rightarrow 0
$$
which noise schedulers $\alpha_t, \beta_t \in [0,1]$ interpolate a data sample with noise, such that
\begin{align*}
    p_0(\cdot|x) &= \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{1}) \\
    p_1(\cdot|x) &= \delta_x .
\end{align*}

Samples from the marginal density $p_t$ can then be easily obtained by interpolating $x_1 \sim p_1$ and $x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)$ via
$$
    x_t = \alpha_t\, x_1 + \beta_t\, x_0 \ \sim\ p_t .
$$
To generate samples that follow this probability path, in flow matching we train a neural network $u_t^\theta$ set to generate $p_t$. 
This is obtained by noting that, although the marginal velocity field is
not immediately available to us, it decomposes as an expectation of conditional fields
$$u_t(x) = \mathbb{E}_{x_1 \sim p_{1\mid t}(\cdot\,\mid x)}\!\big[\,u_t(x \mid x_1)\,\big]$$
each $u_t(\cdot \mid x_1)$ generating a simple path from $p_0$ to a fixed data sample $x_1$. (TODO: repetition) Flow matching
exploits the fact that the tractable conditional objective
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fm-objective}
    \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t,\; x_1 \sim p_1,\; x_t \sim p_t(\cdot\,\mid x_1)}
    \big\lVert u_\theta(x_t, t) - u_t(x_t \mid x_1) \big\rVert^2
\end{equation}
shares its gradient with the intractable marginal one \parencite{lipman2023flow}. Under the linear
(optimal-transport) path $x_t = (1-t)\,x_0 + t\,x_1$ with $x_0 \sim p_0$, the conditional vector field corresponds to the
constant displacement $u_t(x_t \mid x_1) = x_1 - x_0$, such that training amounts to a least-squares
regression onto $x_1 - x_0$.

\section{Guidance}
Guidance methods were originally develped in the context of diffusion models trained to generate images.
In the following we make a short derivation of the guidance under the perspective of (cite DPS paper) and translate it to fit the flow matching framework.
We then discuss the idea of a guidance strength-schedule, and conclude by summarizing other relevant guidance frameworks. 

\subsection{Guidance as posterior sampling}
In most cases, we are not interested in sampling an arbitrary object from $p_1(x)$, but rather to condition the generation on a certain property $y$.
Diffusion posterior sampling \cite{...} (TODO: cite) builds on the idea that guided generation can be framed as sampling from the conditional posterior distribution $p_1(x|y)$. 
The method is derived in short as follows.

Using Bayes rule and taking the gradient with respect to the noisy state we can rewrite the posterior as a sum of scores
\begin{align*}
p_t(z_t | y) &\propto p_t(y | z_t)\, p_t(z_t), \\
\nabla_{z_t}\log p_t(z_t | y) &= \nabla_{z_t}\log p_t(z_t) + \nabla_{z_t}\log p_t(y | z_t).
\end{align*}

In general, $p_t(y\mid z_t)$ is hard to acquire in closed-form due to the dependence on the time $t$, as there only exists explicit dependence between $y$ and $x_1$.
We thus rewrite the intractable likelihood in form of marginal distribution, 
and approximate the two appearing terms inside the integral (also intractable) as follows
\begin{align*}
    p_t(y | z^t) &= \int p_t(y | x, z_t)\, p_t(x | z_t)\, dx \\
    &= \int p_t(y | x)\, p_t(x | z^t)\, dx \qquad y \perp x_t | x \\
    &\approx \int p_t(y | \hat{x}_t)\, p(x | z^t)\, dx \qquad \text{first approximation} \\
    &\approx \int p_t(y | \hat{x}_t)\, \delta_{\hat{x}_t}\,dx \qquad \text{second approximation} \\
    &= p_t(y | \hat{x}_t)
\end{align*}
where $\hat{x}_t$ is the tractable clean estimate at time t.
No conditions are imposed on the approximated likelihood term $p_t(y | \hat{x}_t)$, and can thus be modeled arbitrarily.

\subsection{From diffusion to flow matching}
While the latter expression is immediately applicable to diffusion models, we need to convert the expression first to use it within the flow-matching framework.
For Gaussian probability paths, the unconditional and conditional scores relate to the conditional vector field as follows
\begin{align*}
u(z_t \mid y) &= a_t \nabla_{z_t} \log p_t(z_t\mid y) + b_t\,z_t \\
u_t(z_t) &= a_t \nabla_{z_t} \log p_t(z_t) + b_t\,z_t
\end{align*}

Inserting the \ref{...} (TODO: ref the equation!) allows to reduce the conditional vector field to
\begin{align*}
u(z_t \mid y) &= a_t \nabla_{z_t} (\log p(z_t) + \log p(y \mid z_t)) + b_t\,z_t \\
&= u(z_t) + a_t \nabla_{z_t} \log p(y \mid z_t) .
\end{align*}
The guided vector field can thus be simply expressed as a sum between the unguided vector field and the gradient with respect to the noisy state of the likelihood $p_t(y|z_t)$. 
Finally, by combining this with the approximation \ref{...} (cite eq), we obtain
\begin{align*}
    u_t(z_t \mid y) &= u(z_t) - a_t \nabla_{z_t} \log p(y|z_t) \\
    &\approx u(z_t) - a_t \nabla_{z_t} \log p(y|\hat{x}_t)
\end{align*}

\subsection{Guidance strength-schedule}

While the latter mathematical framework offers an elegant way to express guidance as sampling from the posterior distribution $p_1(x|y)$,
researchers realised that they could obtain better results by scaling up the contribution of the guidance term.
Scaling up the guidance term means that guided samples stop being samples from the posterior distribution, trading mathematical sophistication for control.

To unify the notation we can express this idea as follow: replace $a_t$ with a general guidance strength-schedule $\lambda_t$,
which we can decompose 
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_t := w \cdot \alpha_t \cdot c_t
\end{equation}
with:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item $w$: guidance strength (time independent)
    \item $\alpha_t \in [0,1]$: guidance profile (not necessarily equivalent to $a_t$)
    \item $c_t$: a general step normalization constant (specific to different guidance algorithms)
\end{itemize}

Note that, setting $w=1$, $\{\alpha_t\}_{t=1}^T=\{a_t\}_{t=1}^T$, and $c_t=1$, for $t \in [0,1]$ retrieves the unconditional vector field of \ref{...} (cite eq.).

Under this notation, we define a modified version of the conditional velocity field 
\begin{align*}
    u_t(z_t \mid y) &= u(z_t) - a_t \nabla_{z_t} \log p(y|z_t) \\
    &\approx u(z_t) - a_t \nabla_{z_t} \log p(y|\hat{x}_t) \\
    \tilde{u}_t(z_t\mid y) :&= u(z_t) - \lambda_t \nabla_{z_t} \log p(y|\hat{x}_t)
\end{align*}
denoting it more simply as \emph{guided velocity field}, and rename its counterpart $u(z_t)$ as \emph{unguided velocity field}.

\subsection{Guidance methods}

Guidance scaling approaches to include:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item scale up $w$
    \item change the default profile schedule to an arbitrary $\alpha_t$
    \item normalize the step to satisfy a certain constraint, such as setting $\{\lambda_t\}_{t=1}^T$ s.t. the guidance vector represents a fix fraction of the unguided vector field.
\end{itemize}


In addition to the the choice of different guidance-strength schedules, different approaches model the way the guidance vector is obtained in different ways.

\section{ArchesWeather}
A wide range of generative models have been successfully applied to weather forecasting.
To prototype our method we settle on ArchesWeather \parencite{couairon2024archesweather}, chosen for its low computational cost relative to heavier alternatives such as GenCast \parencite{price2023gencast}.
This section presents the details relevant to our exposition and defers the rest to the original paper.

\subsection{Dataset}
ArchesWeather is trained on the ERA5 reanalysis dataset \parencite{hersbach2020era5}, in one of the versions preprocessed by WeatherBench~2 \parencite{rasp2024weatherbench2}. 

The particular dataset is regridded to a regular $1.5^\circ$ latitude--longitude grid of $240 \times 121$ points.

Each weather state is a stack of $82$ two-dimensional fields. Four of them are surface fields: $2$\,m temperature, mean sea-level pressure, and the two components of the $10$\,m wind. The remaining six are upper-air fields---geopotential, temperature, specific humidity, the two horizontal wind components, and vertical velocity---each resolved at $13$ selected pressure levels
$$
\{50,\,100,\,150,\,200,\,250,\,300,\,400,\,500,\,600,\,700,\,850,\,925,\,1000\}\ \mathrm{hPa},
$$
for a total of $6 \times 13 = 78$ level--field combinations.

Weather states are subsampled at $00$:$00$, $06$:$00$, $12$:$00$ and $18$:$00$\,UTC. Following the WeatherBench model evaluation protocol, all dates from $01.01.2020$ onward are held out as the test set.

\subsection{Objective}

ArchesWeather is a medium range forecaster, predicting up to 15-day trajectories in an autoregressive fashion, and runs two submodels: a deterministic- and a flow-matching-based residual model.
The deterministic model predictics a base forecast $\hat{x}^{\text{det}}_{n+1}$, while the residual model is trained to generate the residual 
$$
    r_{n+1} = x_{n+1} - \hat{x}^{\text{det}}_{n+1}
$$
where $x_{n+1}$ denotes the ground truth state at the next time step.
The two predictions are then combined to form a probabilistic forecast 
$$
    \hat{x}_{n+1} = \hat{x}^{\text{det}}_{n+1} - \hat{r}_{n+1}
$$
This split implies two distinct notions of time (\Cref{fig:time-scales}), which we denote as \emph{weather time} and \emph{flow time}.
The weather time ticks on $24$\,h steps, indexed by $n=0,\dots,N$, with $n=0$ corresponding to the current state and $n=N$ the last forecast step.
The flow time runs within each weather time step and is indexed by $t=0,\dots,T$, with $t=0$ corresponding to pure noise, $t=1$ to the first sampling step, and $t=T$ to the last sampling step (clean residual).

The flow-matching model samples residuals via Gaussian probability paths with noise schedulers $a_t = 1-\frac{t}{T}$ and $b_t=\frac{t}{T}$,
where $s_t:=a_t$ denotes the noise level in the paper's notation.

Instead of regressing the flow-matching velocity $r_{n+1}-\varepsilon$, the flow model is trained to predict the clean residual $\hat{r}_t := r_\theta(z_t,t,c)$ directly via the loss
Writing $\hat{r}_t := r_\theta(z_t,t,c)$ for the clean residual estimate at flow step $t$, the training objective is
$$
    \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t,\,r_{n+1},\,\varepsilon}\big\lVert \hat{r}_t - r_{n+1} \big\rVert^2,
$$
conditioned on $c=(x_{n-1},x_n,\hat{x}^{\text{det}}_{n+1})$, and $z_t$ being the noisy residual equivalent to 
$$
    z_t = (1-s_t)\,r_{n+1} + s_t\,\varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I).
$$


Both models operate directly in normalized data space, so the noise, the residual and the state share the same $240 \times 121$ grid across the $82$ fields.
We defer the details of the two models' architectures to the original paper.

\subsection{Sampling}
At sampling time the residual prediction is turned back into a flow-matching velocity.
Inverting the interpolation for the implied noise $\hat{\varepsilon}_t$ and forming the displacement $\hat{r}_t-\hat{\varepsilon}_t$ gives
\begin{align*}
    \hat{\varepsilon}_t &= \frac{z_t - (1-s_t)\,\hat{r}_t}{s_t}, \\
    u_\theta(z_t,t) &= \hat{r}_t - \hat{\varepsilon}_t \\
                    &= \frac{s_t\,\hat{r}_t - \big(z_t - (1-s_t)\,\hat{r}_t\big)}{s_t} \\
                    &= \frac{\hat{r}_t - z_t}{s_t}.
\end{align*}
The residual is sampled by integrating the velocity over $T=25$ Euler steps
$$
    z_{t+1} = z_t + h_t\,u_\theta(z_t,t), \qquad h_t = s_t - s_{t+1},
$$
with $\hat{r}_{n+1}:=z_T$,
such that the combined forecast is equivalent to
$$
    \hat{x}_{n+1} = \hat{x}^{\text{det}}_{n+1} + \hat{r}_{n+1}=\hat{x}^{\text{det}}_{n+1} + z_T.
$$
Sampling multiple residuals is then a cheap way to produce ensemble forecasts.
