\chapter{Specifying events of interest}\label{ch:specify}

In this chapter we expose how we choose to specify events of interest, that is, how we set the guidance target.

At this point it is important to recall that in this thesis we narrow the focus on heatwaves, and will present the methods accordingly.
However, the method we develop is variable agnostic (and model agnostic), and could be tuned to generate other types of extreme events, such as hurricanes, flods, etc., following a very similar logic.

As previewed in the introduction, we are interested in guiding the base forecasts by targeting a trajectory of masked averages over the selected variable and region of interest. 
In our specific use case, we target an increase in the average temperature over a certain region, over $N$ weather steps.

We will spend this chapter explaining how we define the relevant knobs (target parameters) of our heuristic
and conclude by examining the induced objects and their relations.


\section{Target variable and region: mask}

We define the region of interest as a simple bounding box around pixels in the $121\times240$ rectangular spatial grid.
A pixel is either included or excluded from the mask.

This approach has three benefits. 
Firstly, magnitudes of different objects are comparable across mask sizes.
Secondly, it enables us to efficiently compute any average of choice inside the mask region.
While the mask has non-zero weigths only at the target variable field, the mask region can be used to compute arbitrary objects, such as the average of other variables but not only, that have the same longitudinal and latitudinal coordinates as the target variable.
Finally, defining a region with equal weights inside the mask avoids biasing the guidance inside the region, making it clear what area we are targeting in the first place.
Take the opposite for understanding: if we defined a Bell-shaped mask, it wouldn't be clear what region we are spefing exactly, and we would be biasing the generation in un unpredictable manner, making it difficult to predict different across shapes. 

Every pixel is weighted in proportion to the area it covers, so that the masked average is a true area average and not biased towards the poles. 
This is the latitude weighting scheme of WeatherBench 2~\parencite{rasp2024weatherbench2}, also used in the model's training loss.

We index grid pixels by $i\in\{1,\dots,121\}$ in the latitude direction and $j\in\{1,\dots,240\}$ in the longitude direction, which give us coordinates $(\lambda_j,\varphi_i)$.
We denote the box indicator by $k_{ij}$ ($1$ inside the region, $0$ outside), and cell area by $a_i$ at latitude $\varphi_i$. The pixel weights are then
$$
m(\lambda_j,\varphi_i)=\frac{k_{ij}\,a_i}{\sum_{i',j'} k_{i'j'}\,a_{i'}},\qquad a_i=\int_{\beta_i^{-}}^{\beta_i^{+}}\cos\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\varphi,
$$
where $[\beta_i^{-},\beta_i^{+}]$ is the latitude band of row $i$, so that $a_i$ is the exact area of the cell. 
As a consequence cells at the equator now (correctly) carry more weight than ones at the poles.

\section{Target trajectory}

Once we have specified the variable and region of interest, we still have to define the target mask-averaged values, which we want to guide the model towards:
a scalar trajectory over $N$ weather steps.

But how can we specify valid target trajectories in the sense of climatologically plausible ones? 
The answer really depends on the specific use case we have in mind, which would call for different heuristics.
We might come up with arbitrary sampling strategy based on historical trajectories that we can query from the dataset at hand.

To simplify matters, in this thesis we define target trajectories as a linear percentage increase from the current state to a prescribed maximum w.r.t. base forecast at a specified step $N-H$.
Concretly, we prescribe the target trajectory $\{y_n\}_{n=1}^N$ as follows:
\begin{align}
    y_n &= \rho_n \cdot \mathcal{M}(x^\text{ung}_n) \\
    \rho_n&= \frac{\rho_{N-H}}{N} \quad \text{if } n \leq N-H \text{ else } 0
\end{align}

Defining the trajectory using the percentage approach enables us to analyze different levels of intensities across different settings more easily.
What we are in fact doing though, is defining the target temperatures.

Explicitly defining a target trajectory allows us to separates clearly the target of the guidance, and the search for optimal parameters of the guidance method.
While the papers summarized in chapter 2 all play around with different guidance strengths to achieve different results, we take an orthogonal approach:
we prescribe different guidance objectives, and then retrieve the most adequate parameters that bring us there in a separate step.
These two definitions are really equivalent in terms of the retrieved parameters and the produced outcome under the same guidance framework, however,
the latter approach has following benefit: 
if we we can define desired outcomes a priori, we do not have to tune parameters and discard experiments that do not achieve desired results, we can optimize them directly.

This simple realization is the main contribution of our work. The rest of the contributions are developed as a consequence.
